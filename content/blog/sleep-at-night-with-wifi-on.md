It's a common habit: phone on the nightstand, Wi-Fi router humming in the corner, smart devices scattered through the bedroom. But as wireless technology saturates our living spaces, a question persists: does sleeping in an electromagnetic field affect our health?

## The Science: What We Know

### Non-Ionizing Radiation

Wi-Fi operates at 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz: non-ionizing radiofrequency (RF) radiation. Unlike X-rays or UV light, it lacks energy to directly damage DNA. Regulatory bodies (WHO, ICNIRP, FCC) set exposure limits based on thermal effects: tissue heating at high power densities.

### Current Evidence

**World Health Organization (WHO):** "To date, no adverse health effects have been established as being caused by mobile phone use" (extending to Wi-Fi at typical exposures).

**ICNIRP Guidelines:** Set limits 50x below levels showing any biological effect in research. Typical home Wi-Fi exposes users to 0.1–1% of these limits.

**Recent Studies:** Some research suggests possible non-thermal effects at low levels: oxidative stress, melatonin disruption, blood-brain barrier permeability. But results are inconsistent, often at higher exposures than typical home environments, and mechanisms remain theoretical.

### The Precautionary Principle

Absence of evidence ≠ evidence of absence. Long-term, low-dose, multi-source exposure hasn't been extensively studied. Some countries (France, Israel, Cyprus) apply precautionary policies: banning Wi-Fi in nurseries, preferring wired connections in schools.

## Practical Concerns Beyond Radiation

### Sleep Disruption (The Real Culprit)

The biggest health impact isn't RF radiation: it's the device itself:

- **Blue light** suppresses melatonin, delaying sleep onset
- **Notifications** fragment sleep architecture
- **Psychological arousal** from checking email/social media
- **FOMO-driven checking** creates anticipatory anxiety

Studies consistently show: screen use before bed → poorer sleep quality, shorter duration, next-day cognitive impairment.

### Security Risks

An always-on network exposes attack surface:
- Unpatched IoT devices (cameras, bulbs, speakers)
- Router vulnerabilities (often never updated)
- Neighbor access to poorly secured networks
- Data exfiltration from compromised devices

## Practical Recommendations

### For Radiation Concerns (Low Effort)

1. **Distance is your friend** - Move router out of bedroom; inverse-square law dramatically reduces exposure
2. **Timer outlets** - Schedule router shutdown 11 PM – 6 AM (saves power too)
3. **Wired where it matters** - Ethernet for desktop, TV, gaming; reserve Wi-Fi for mobile devices
4. **Airplane mode** - Phone on nightstand = airplane mode (or keep it outside bedroom)

### For Actual Sleep Quality (High Impact)

1. **Digital sunset** - Screens off 60 minutes before sleep
2. **Blue light filters** - f.lux, Night Shift, or blue-blocking glasses if screens unavoidable
3. **Charge outside bedroom** - Eliminates temptation and notification disruption
4. **Analog alarm clock** - Removes "just check the time" phone pickup
5. **Consistent schedule** - Same sleep/wake times, even weekends

### For Network Security (Essential)

1. **WPA3 encryption** - Or WPA2-AES minimum; never WEP/WPA
2. **Change default admin credentials** - Immediately on router setup
3. **Disable WPS, UPnP, remote management** - Common attack vectors
4. **Separate IoT network** - Guest VLAN for smart devices
5. **Firmware updates** - Monthly check or auto-update if available
6. **Strong unique passwords** - 12+ characters for Wi-Fi and admin

## The Bottom Line

**Wi-Fi radiation at typical home exposures:** No established health risk per current science, but long-term gaps exist. Precautionary measures are low-cost and reasonable.

**Sleeping with devices and poor network hygiene:** Documented, significant negative impacts on sleep, cognition, and security.

**Best investment:** Focus on sleep hygiene and network security. If radiation concerns persist, distance and scheduled shutdown address them at near-zero cost.

Your router isn't the enemy: your bedtime scrolling is.
